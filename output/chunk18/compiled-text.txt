Zero Trust Architecture with Bias Risk and
Grounding Controls for AI Interview Systems
Sai Pranavi A P, Sai Triveni B, Sanjana H V, and Sneha V
Department of AI & ML, RV College of Engineering
Bengaluru, India
Abstract—AI interview systems must govern access to can-
didate records and the release of model-generated feedback.
Authorization alone does not establish that an assessment is
supported or free from discriminatory treatment. This paper
presents TrustInterview AI, an architecture that separates access
assurance from output assurance and couples their decisions
at assessment transitions. The access plane checks provisioned
identity, role, tenant, ownership and session validity. The output
plane checks source provenance, selected sensitive-content sig-
nals, claim support and control availability before release, review
or abstention. A controlled reference implementation provides
server-owned assessments, immutable resume evidence, trans-
actional persistence and authorized review. Sixty-nine backend
tests and twelve analysis-tool tests pass; a forty-eight-case ofﬂine
pilot veriﬁes experiment recording without estimating efﬁcacy.
The implemented claim checker handles a bounded technology-
use grammar, while an optional model adapter remains unver-
iﬁed with a live provider. We specify independent annotation
and paired demographic experiments to evaluate the remaining
coverage and fairness questions. These results establish selected
enforcement behaviours, not general hallucination reduction or
demographic fairness.
Index Terms—Zero Trust Architecture, AI interviews, bias
risk, source grounding, output assurance, policy enforcement
I. INTRODUCTION
An AI interview workﬂow handles several kinds of au-
thority. A candidate may submit an answer, an application
may retrieve a resume, a model may propose feedback, and a
reviewer may approve its release. These actions cross different
trust boundaries. A valid session can request another candi-
date’s record, while an authorized model request can return
an unsupported assessment. A detector warning can also be
ineffective when the application persists or displays the warned
output. The resulting research problem concerns the conditions
under which an artifact may move through the workﬂow, rather
than the presence of security middleware alone.
Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) provides a resource-centred
basis for access decisions. NIST SP 800-207 requires the
system to avoid treating network location as sufﬁcient trust
and to evaluate access to protected resources [1]. Applying
this principle to an interview requires server-side subject–
resource authorization, including the candidate’s ownership
of an assessment. However, ZTA does not by itself evaluate
factual support or hiring bias. We therefore introduce an
application-level output-assurance plane alongside the access
plane. A model response becomes input to a release decision,
not authoritative evidence of its own correctness.
The output problem has two distinct forms. A model can
invent a qualiﬁcation or recommendation that the supplied
evidence does not support. Separately, a candidate’s statement
can differ from the resume. The latter is a source discrepancy,
not automatically a model hallucination or proof of dishon-
esty. A resume is candidate-supplied material and does not
independently verify qualiﬁcations. Bias introduces another
distinction: mentioning a sensitive attribute is different from
treating otherwise comparable candidates differently. Both
source grounding and sensitive-content screening therefore
require explicit evidence boundaries.
A. Objective and Contributions
We investigate how resource authorization and output-
release controls can be coupled in an AI interview without
collapsing them into one risk score. The proposed contribution
is an enforceable architecture: each plane produces an explicit
decision, and assessment state records whether the result is
released, held, rejected or withheld. Our work has three parts:
1) A threat model and dual-policy architecture connect-
ing identity, resource ownership, bias-risk evidence and
grounding at interview-workﬂow transitions.
2) A controlled prototype implementing selected access and
output invariants, including immutable source binding,
transactional assessment state and reasoned human re-
view.
3) A reproducible veriﬁcation artifact and a prepared an-
notation and counterfactual protocol that distinguish
conformance tests from efﬁcacy measurements.
The paper is a systems and architecture study with pre-
liminary implementation veriﬁcation. We do not claim a new
general-purpose fairness or hallucination detector. The current
score is a narrow keyword ﬁxture, not a validated measure
of interview competence. An optional live-provider adapter
has been implemented but tested only with transport ﬁxtures.
The remaining empirical questions concern semantic cover-
age, group differences in release burden and actual provider
behaviour. They are stated as evaluation requirements rather
than reported ﬁndings.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Zero Trust and Generative AI Risk
NIST SP 800-207 identiﬁes a policy decision point (PDP)
and a policy enforcement point (PEP) for resource access [1].

SP 800-207A extends the access-control discussion to cloud-
native and multi-cloud applications [2]. These sources mo-
tivate checking subject, resource and context at an applica-
tion boundary. They do not deﬁne a hiring-fairness test or
establish the semantic correctness of a model assessment.
NIST’s Generative AI Proﬁle separately identiﬁes risks requir-
ing evaluation and monitoring, including confabulation and
harmful bias [3]. Our architecture connects these concerns
operationally while retaining their different decision criteria.
B. Bias in Hiring Workﬂows
Research on language-model hiring decisions evaluates de-
mographic effects using controlled candidate comparisons [4].
Name-based employment recommendations also illustrate why
demographic signals can affect outputs even when qualiﬁca-
tions are comparable [5]. JobFair provides a framework for
benchmarking gender hiring bias [6]; other job–resume match-
ing work examines gender, race and education [7]. Resume
language itself can carry demographic signals, complicating a
simple removal strategy [8].
These studies motivate paired experiments and subgroup
error analysis. A lexical screen can ﬂag a quotation containing
a selected sensitive term, but cannot determine whether a
hiring decision is discriminatory. It can miss indirect language
and can burden candidates who disclose an attribute without
making it job-relevant. We use such ﬁndings to trigger review
and reserve the term fairness for a separate comparison of
outcomes under controlled variations.
C. Grounding and Adversarial Evidence
Hallucination research distinguishes generated claims, evi-
dence and evaluation methods [9]. SelfCheckGPT uses con-
sistency across samples to identify potentially unreliable
text [10]; consistency alone does not establish that a claim
about a candidate follows from a resume. Our source-relative
formulation instead asks whether a particular statement is sup-
ported, contradicted or undetermined by the supplied evidence,
without treating that evidence as independently true.
Candidate material is also an adversarial input boundary.
Resume prompt-injection studies examine attempts to redi-
rect automated screening [11], [12], while OWASP identiﬁes
prompt injection among application risks [13]. Accordingly,
candidate text cannot change role, ownership, rubric or release
policy. The provider receives evidence as data, and the server
validates returned artifacts. Prompt instructions complement
this enforcement but do not replace it.
D. Position of This Work
Our contribution is the coupling between resource autho-
rization and evidence-based artifact release. Access-control
literature, hiring-bias benchmarks and grounding methods in-
form different parts of this design. The prototype makes their
interaction visible through server-owned state and auditable
outcomes. Its novelty must be assessed at this architectural
boundary, rather than inferred from a count of middleware
layers or conﬁgured detector categories. General semantic

III. TERMINOLOGY AND THREAT MODEL
A. Actors, Assets and Assumptions
The protected assets are resume text, answers, assessment
state, scores, proposed feedback and review records. Candi-
dates access their own assessments within an assigned tenant;
reviewers inspect held assessments within their tenant. The
browser, candidate material and model responses are untrusted
inputs. The backend policy implementation, provisioned prin-
cipal registry and authoritative store form the trusted com-
puting base. Compromise of these components is outside the
prototype’s demonstrated protection.
Our reference deployment is a single backend instance
with exclusive ownership of its SQLite database. Sessions are
volatile and require new authentication after restart. Identity
can be provisioned locally or mapped from a veriﬁed external
subject, but real Google sign-in has not been exercised. Tenant
and role bindings remain server-owned. No biometric identity,
proctoring or identity inference from names is included.
B. Output Deﬁnitions
Source-supported means the available evidence supports a
statement in the declared scope. Source-contradicted requires
explicit incompatible evidence in the same scope. Undeter-
mined covers missing, insufﬁcient, ambiguous or conﬂicting
evidence. Absence of a technology in a resume is not con-
tradiction, and a supported resume quotation is not external
qualiﬁcation veriﬁcation. Model-origin unsupported assertions
and candidate-origin source inconsistencies are recorded sep-
arately.
Bias-risk evidence is a signal for inspection, not a discrim-
ination ﬁnding. In the prototype, selected sensitive-attribute
terms and instruction-like phrases are deterministic review
triggers. Their vocabulary and contextual limits are explicit.
Operational abstention is also separate from the semantic label
undetermined: a provider failure can cause abstention without
producing a claim label.
Table I identiﬁes workﬂow threats rather than an exhaustive
attack taxonomy. An attacker may reuse a stolen bearer token

until expiry or revocation; the prototype does not demonstrate
device binding or resistance to a compromised browser. Like-
wise, database hashes provide local consistency checks, not
protection against an administrator rewriting the store and its
audit history. These limits deﬁne which threat claims the tests
can support.
IV. DUAL POLICY ZERO TRUST ARCHITECTURE
A. Decision Separation
Figure 1 shows the two assurance planes. The access plane
decides whether a principal may act on a resource. The output
plane decides whether the resulting artifact may be released.
The latter is an application policy extension, not a claim
that NIST deﬁnes fairness as an access-control property. We
express the decisions as
DA = fA(I, R, T, O, S, X),
(1)
DO = fO(A, E, B, G, P, H),
(2)
where I is identity, R role, T tenant, O ownership, S session
status and X assessment context. For output, A is the pro-
posed artifact, E its bound evidence, B bias-risk signals, G
grounding results, P provenance and H control availability.
A release transition requires valid access and an acceptable
output decision. Neither a successful provider call nor a
detector score grants resource authority.
The architectural access outcomes include allow, deny, step-
up and revoke. The current reference service implements al-
low/deny with expiry and revocation; interactive step-up is not
implemented. The output design admits release, regeneration,
abstention, review and denial. The implemented path releases
valid output, holds selected ﬁndings for review, or abstains;
regeneration is not implemented. Stating these differences
avoids presenting every conceptual policy outcome as an
existing feature.
B. Resource and Session Enforcement
The backend resolves a principal from a provisioned cre-
dential or trusted identity adapter. It issues an opaque session
token and keeps expiry and revocation state on the server.
Protected routes require that token. For candidates, every
assessment operation checks role, tenant and owner; reviewers
may inspect only held resources in their tenant. Inaccessible
resources return a non-disclosing resource error. A role string
or user identiﬁer submitted in JSON cannot replace these
bindings.
Assessments are created with a server-owned question and
rubric. Submission accepts an answer rather than a client
score, and begins a versioned transition from awaiting-answer
to evaluating. Concurrent or repeated submissions cannot
overwrite committed state. After asynchronous provider work,
session validity is checked again before release. A revoked or
expired session cannot gain a scored result simply because the
provider completed successfully.
The external identity adapter uses browser-bound state,
nonce, Proof Key for Code Exchange (PKCE) and a server-
assigned subject mapping following Google OpenID Connect
documentation [14]. Signed local test assertions exercise cryp-
tographic validation and negative claims; code exchange and
key retrieval are ﬁxtures. This establishes selected protocol
behaviour, not successful live authentication. Reviewer access
still derives from the server’s binding rather than provider-
supplied role text.
C. Source Binding and Grounding
An optional plain-text resume is attached once before sub-
mission. The input is bounded to 8000 UTF-8 bytes, rejects
unexpected authority ﬁelds and invalid text, and belongs to
the candidate’s assessment. The store records its text and
SHA-256 digest with a correlated audit event. The digest
is checked before provider invocation and enters the cache
context. Evidence is copied for the provider, preventing its
in-process mutation from becoming authoritative source state.
PDF ingestion and OCR are not implemented in this controlled
path.
The feedback contract permits bounded exact quotations
linked to answer, question or resume. Unknown source identi-
ﬁers, fabricated quotations and unexpected output ﬁelds cause
abstention. This is structural provenance checking: a quoted
falsehood can pass, a relevant phrase can omit context, and
correct paraphrases cannot pass a quotation-only contract.
Accordingly, quote matching is never reported as general
semantic hallucination detection.
The current claim checker adds a deliberately limited source
comparison. It recognizes whole-line ﬁrst-person technology-
use statements and their explicit negations, using a ﬁxed
alias vocabulary. Same-polarity evidence is source-supported;
opposite-polarity evidence is source-contradicted. Missing as-
sertions, conﬂicting statements, unknown technologies and un-
supported wording remain undetermined. Java and JavaScript
are distinct, while aliases such as JS and JavaScript are
explicitly mapped. General prose, dates, duration and com-
petence require a broader semantic method. When no claim is
extracted, the input is outside the checker’s recognized scope;
it is not veriﬁed.
D. Bias-Risk Routing and Release
The output screen checks quoted text for selected sensitive-
attribute and instruction-like phrases using word boundaries.
A ﬁnding routes the output to review rather than asserting
discrimination. This prevents certain ﬂagged artifacts from
being silently released but leaves indirect language, context
and vocabulary coverage unresolved. Source provenance is
retained so that a candidate disclosure is not automatically
treated as a model’s demographic judgment.

E. Persistence, Audit and Failure Handling
Assessment mutation and its audit event commit in one
SQLite transaction. Compare-and-swap version checks pre-
vent stale updates. A crash during provider evaluation leaves
durable evaluating state; startup recovery changes interrupted
assessments to abstained and removes uncommitted result
ﬁelds. Exclusive database ownership prevents a second in-
stance from recovering another active instance’s work. The
design is a single-instance reference implementation, not a
distributed availability claim.
Audit records contain policy version, correlated request,
action and decision, hashed principal references and selected
source/review hashes. A hash chain detects some local al-
terations but lacks external anchoring and cannot prevent
privileged rewriting or valid-preﬁx truncation. Resume and
answer evidence remain plaintext in the database. Sessions and
caches are volatile. Cache writes follow durable release and
use candidate/session/assessment/source context; cache reuse
and eviction have not been evaluated.
V. PROTOTYPE IMPLEMENTATION AND BOUNDARIES
A. Historical Prototype and Controlled Reference
The historical INTERVIEW-BOT and TrustInterview-AI
repositories supply implementation context, not the ﬁnal re-
search evidence. A code review identiﬁed anonymous session
issuance, inconsistent ownership checks, client-authoritative
scores, shared question context, route-dependent detector be-
haviour and simulated interface values. Missing resume evi-
dence could also be interpreted as veriﬁed by a heuristic path.
These observations motivated an isolated reference boundary
rather than a claim that the original middleware already
established ZTA.
The controlled backend is the default entry point and serves
a dedicated candidate/reviewer interface. Historical routes re-
main available only through an explicit legacy entry point
and are not mounted by the controlled service. Table III
summarizes what has been implemented and what remains
limited. Backend and analysis sources, test reports and ver-
sioned input ﬁles are retained with SHA-256 manifests. The
long traceability review and test logs are supporting artifacts;
their detail is not counted as a separate research contribution
for each remediation step.
The ﬁxture score is the rounded percentage of the terms
identity, authorization and resource occurring as words in
the answer. Term presence, rather than repetition or resume
content, determines this value. This transparent rule permits
reproducible authority tests but supplies no evidence of com-
petence.
B. Model Adapter and Recording Protocol
The optional Gemini REST adapter uses a ﬁxed service end-
point and privately conﬁgured credentials. It transmits bounded
evidence, requests a constrained JSON schema and invokes
no tools. Timeouts abort requests; HTTP errors, blocked or
incomplete responses, malformed JSON and oversized bodies
fail closed. Live mode has no automatic ofﬂine fallback. The
default provider remains an explicitly labelled ofﬂine ﬁxture.
The format follows the provider documentation [15]; ﬁxture
tests do not establish live API interoperability or reliability.
A sequential experiment runner uses a bounded seeded plan
and a fresh output directory. It records input and prompt-

C. Research Questions and Study Conﬁgurations
The efﬁcacy study asks four questions. RQ1 asks whether
resource and transition controls prevent unauthorized reads and
scored releases under adversarial requests. RQ2 asks how ac-
curately the grounding method distinguishes supported, contra-
dicted and undetermined claims, including extraction failures.

RQ3 asks whether output decisions and error burdens change
across qualiﬁcation-preserving demographic pairs. RQ4 asks
what latency, failure and review cost the coupled controls
introduce under a speciﬁed workload.
The proposed conﬁgurations isolate contributions: C0 is a
declared baseline, C1 adds access assurance, C2 adds output
assurance, C3 combines both, and C4 adds the selected ad-
vanced semantic method. They must share the same provider,
rubric, input set and decoding settings when the comparison
requires them. The historical timing conﬁgurations are compo-
nent analogues, not evidence that these live system ablations
have been run. Any control disabled for a synthetic experiment
remains separate from a deployed protected workﬂow.
D. Prepared Corpora and Independent Annotation
The synthetic procedure pilot has twenty-four grounding ex-
amples and twelve two-member demographic pairs. Grounding
cases include negation, omission, aliases, quantities, conﬂict-
ing assertions, dates, duration, reported speech and compe-
tence inference. Claim origin distinguishes candidate source
discrepancy from unsupported model assertions. The cases
are authored inputs, not observed provider outputs. Annota-
tors judge general semantic support beyond the implemented
grammar, allowing coverage gaps to appear in the evaluation.
Pairs cover three junior technical roles and four declared
signals: pronouns, age, marital status and disclosed disability.
Within each pair, the job, technical evidence and answer
are identical. These signals require faculty review before a
larger study and do not represent a population’s distribution or
legitimate hiring criteria. Two differently shufﬂed packets omit
predictions, pair identiﬁers and author categories. Labels and
adjudication ﬁelds remain blank; the pilot validates procedure
and will be excluded from the ﬁnal held-out test set.
Two human reviewers independently label claims as source-
supported, source-contradicted or undetermined, citing exact
evidence spans and recording ambiguity. Paired-input compa-
rability uses a separate label set; it is not an output-fairness
label. A third reviewer resolves disagreement and ﬂagged
ambiguity after original annotations are frozen. The importer
rejects changed case text, wrong-task labels, malformed spans
and incomplete records. Distinct reviewer identiﬁers cannot
prove human independence; faculty must verify that procedure.
Agreement, ambiguity counts and evidence-span agreement
are reported before adjudication, with undeﬁned statistics left
explicitly unset.
E. Metrics, Analysis and Reproducibility
Security evaluation counts unauthorized transitions, replay
acceptance, cross-tenant leakage, tampered-score acceptance
and audited decisions. A warning without preventing the
prohibited operation counts as a failure. Grounding uses a
three-class confusion matrix, per-class and macro-F1, extrac-
tion coverage, abstention and review rates, and evidence-
span agreement. Candidate-origin and model-origin claims are
reported separately. Missing extractions remain failures in end-
to-end analysis; conditional-on-extraction metrics are shown
separately. An empty extracted subset receives no successful
accuracy value.
Fairness analysis compares paired output scores only when
the rubric is meaningful and the scores are available. For
the current constrained workﬂow, the relevant comparisons
are release, abstention and review burden, along with group-
speciﬁc grounding errors. Missing or held scores are not
replaced by zero. Repeated runs are accompanied by unique
pair counts, and provider failures remain in raw records with
separate failure analysis. Ranking or selection-rate claims
require a deﬁned ranking task and a predeclared selection
threshold; neither is evaluated here.
For a ﬁnal powered study, sample size, primary outcomes
and analysis rules must be ﬁxed before collection. The protocol
calls for uncertainty intervals, paired analysis and multiplicity
correction where appropriate; the present descriptive utility
does not implement population inference. Pilot cases used to
estimate variance or reﬁne instructions must not enter the ﬁnal
test set. A zero observed failure count must be accompanied
by its sample size and uncertainty, not a claim of zero risk.
Each report records source/input hashes, case identiﬁers,
model/conﬁguration metadata, raw response references, deci-
sions and error states. Raw artifacts must be reviewed without
exposing credentials or applicant data. Tables for efﬁcacy will
be generated from frozen annotations and prediction exports
after collection. No such tables are populated in this paper. At
present, the completed evidence consists of implementation
veriﬁcation and an ofﬂine recording protocol, with no live
model calls or independent efﬁcacy labels.
VII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
The strongest current result is an inspectable release bound-
ary. A browser cannot authoritatively select a tenant, reviewer
role or assessment score. A model cannot add a score ﬁeld,
cite an absent source or bypass a failed durable commit
while remaining within the demonstrated workﬂow. Selected
sensitive signals and source contradictions create an auditable
review transition. This makes the interaction between access
authority and output authority concrete, which is the principal
architectural contribution.
The checks leave substantial semantic limitations. Quotation
matching veriﬁes a substring relationship, not entailment, ex-
ternal truth, salience or completeness. The controlled grammar
misses general resume prose and does not model changes over
time. A quoted sensitive disclosure may cause unnecessary
review, whereas indirect discriminatory language can escape
the trigger vocabulary. A reviewer can authorize an artifact
without establishing its fairness. Consequently, neither test
counts nor the number of rules supports a claim that bias or
hallucination has been eliminated.
Operational generalization is also limited. The reference
backend is single-instance, sessions are memory-resident, ev-
idence is plaintext, and the audit chain is not externally
anchored. The optional provider has not been exercised live,
and timeout or model-version effects have not been measured
in deployment. The synthetic pilot contains no real applicants

and only selected language, roles and demographic signals.
Any later use of candidate data requires institutional approval,
appropriate participation and data minimization, with a process
to contest outcomes. The current score must not be used for
autonomous employment decisions.
For faculty review, the architecture, code boundary and
preliminary veriﬁcation form a complete description of the
present artifact. A stronger empirical submission requires
independent annotation, a meaningful task rubric and recorded
live provider outputs. These are missing research evidence, not
formatting details. Further claims should follow that evidence
rather than be inferred from a working interface or a successful
API integration.
VIII. CONCLUSION
The TrustInterview AI architecture couples resource access
and feedback release through server-owned assessment state.
It binds candidate evidence, validates constrained provider
artifacts, supports reasoned review and preserves selected fail-
ure transitions transactionally. Veriﬁcation records 69 passing
backend tests, ﬁve interface tests and twelve analysis-tool tests.
The 48 ofﬂine pilot records establish recording and importer
compatibility. These ﬁndings concern selected workﬂow be-
haviours; general semantic grounding, demographic fairness,
competence scoring and live-provider reliability remain unval-
idated. Independent annotation and paired model experiments
are required to test whether the architecture improves AI
interview outcomes beyond enforcing its explicit boundaries.
